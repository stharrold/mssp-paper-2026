Dear Editor,

Please consider the research article "Early renewal of accountable care organization agreements to reset benchmarks
in the Medicare Shared Savings Program: a preregistered retrospective cohort study" for *BMC Health Services
Research*, for the collection "Implementing value-based care payment models in diverse health systems".

The Medicare Shared Savings Program is Medicare's largest value-based payment model, and its benchmarks decide whether
an accountable care organization earns shared savings. The study evaluates a design feature of that model: the 2019
rules let an organization end its agreement early and reset its benchmark, which was also the only way for an
organization in the BASIC track to reach the ENHANCED track's 75% share of savings without waiting for its agreement
to end or leaving the program. Twenty-nine of 334 eligible
organizations did so in 2022. An industry report described them; to my knowledge no peer-reviewed study has examined
why they renewed early.

The paper responds to the collection's call for work on the design, implementation, adaptation and evaluation of
value-based care payment models, and to its topic of the policy reforms and governance that support implementation. It
examines how participating organizations responded
once a national program's redesigned rules were in force: few of those eligible took the early reset, and for most of
those who did, renewal also meant moving to a higher share of savings. The study covers one program, in the United
States, but the trade-off it documents applies to any shared-savings model that resets benchmarks on an organization's
own past spending, whichever health system runs it.

The findings bear on how the model's rules sort participants. The intuitive account, renewing early to raise the
benchmark, does not hold on average: early renewers were already beating their benchmarks, so the own-spending part
of a reset offset its regional gain, and their published 2022 benchmarks, examined post hoc, averaged 1.3% below the
year before. For 26 of 27 early renewers in the BASIC track, renewal was instead the move to the higher sharing rate,
the trade the rules were built around; the design cannot separate the two motives, and the paper says so. Spending
growth afterward did not differ detectably. The results give a baseline for the rules in force since 2024, which make
a reset cheaper for organizations that have saved.

The methods are built for checking. The plan, decision rules and code were registered on OSF Registries
(doi:10.17605/OSF.IO/C53W9) before any outcome data were read, and the code enforced that order. The benchmark Medicare
does not publish was rebuilt from the regulation and validated before use. All data are public, and the code, data
snapshot and results are archived, so every estimate can be rerun. Every registered conclusion agreed with its
conventional frequentist counterpart. The manuscript follows STROBE (Additional file 1).

Dear Editor,

Please consider the manuscript "Early Renewal of Accountable Care Organization Agreements to Reset Benchmarks in the Medicare Shared Savings Program: Preregistered Retrospective Cohort Study" for the Interactive Journal of Medical Research as an Original Paper.

In the Medicare Shared Savings Program, benchmarks decide whether an accountable care organization earns shared savings and shape which organizations join, stay and save. Earlier studies found that regional benchmarking shifted participation toward providers with low spending for their regions, and that organizations whose benchmarks fell were more likely to leave. The 2019 rules added a third choice: ending an agreement early to reset the benchmark sooner. Twenty-nine of 334 eligible organizations did so in 2022. An industry report described them, but to my knowledge no peer-reviewed study has examined who used the option.

The study makes two substantive contributions. First, the intuitive account, renewing early to raise the benchmark, does not hold on average: early renewers were already beating their benchmarks, so the own-spending part of a reset offset its regional gain, and their 2022 benchmarks, examined post hoc, averaged 1.3% below the year before. For 26 of 27 early renewers in the BASIC track, renewal was instead the move to a 75% sharing rate, the trade the rules were built around; the pattern fits that motive better, though the design cannot separate the two. Second, early renewers were sorted on the positions found for joining and staying, which extends that literature to the timing of resets. Spending growth afterward did not differ detectably. The findings give a baseline for the 2024 rules, which cheapen resets for organizations that have saved.

The methods are the third contribution. The plan, decision rules and code were registered on OSF Registries (osf.io/c53w9) before any outcome data were read, and the code enforced that order. The unpublished counterfactual benchmark was rebuilt from the regulation and validated before use. The registered estimates are Bayesian, with permutation and simulation tests, and every registered conclusion agreed with its conventional frequentist counterpart, so the conclusions do not depend on the framework.

Dear Editor,

Please consider the manuscript "Early Renewal of Accountable Care Organization Agreements to Reset Benchmarks in the Medicare Shared Savings Program: Preregistered Retrospective Cohort Study" for the Interactive Journal of Medical Research as an Original Paper.

In the Medicare Shared Savings Program, benchmarks decide whether an accountable care organization earns shared savings and shape which organizations join, stay and save. Earlier studies found that regional benchmarking shifted participation toward providers with low spending relative to their regions, and that organizations whose benchmarks fell were more likely to leave. The 2019 rules added a third choice: ending an agreement early to reset the benchmark sooner. Twenty-nine of 334 eligible organizations did so in 2022. An industry report has described them, but to my knowledge who used this option has not been studied in peer-reviewed work.

The study makes three contributions. First, it extends the evidence that participation follows the benchmark formula from whether organizations join or leave to when they reset: early renewers were already low-cost relative to their regions and, in the BASIC track, more often bearing two-sided risk, for whom renewal was the only route to a 75% sharing rate. Second, it finds that the intuitive account, renewing early to raise the benchmark, does not hold on average: the own-spending part of a reset offset its regional gain, and their 2022 benchmarks, examined post hoc, averaged 1.3% below the year before. Third, spending growth after renewal did not differ detectably. The findings also give a baseline for evaluating the 2024 rules, which credit prior savings.

The methods are part of the contribution. The plan, decision rules and code were registered on OSF Registries (osf.io/c53w9) before any outcome data were read, and the code enforced that order. The unpublished counterfactual benchmark was rebuilt from the regulation and validated before use. The registered estimates are Bayesian, with permutation and simulation tests and a decision model that carries the rebuilt benchmark's measurement error; every registered conclusion agreed with its conventional frequentist counterpart, so the conclusions do not depend on the inferential framework.
